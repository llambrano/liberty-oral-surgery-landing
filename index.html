Do my wisdom teeth need to come out?

Teeth that come in straight and healthy can often stay. Teeth that are impacted — tilted, trapped in the jaw, or only partly through the gums — usually cause trouble sooner or later. These are the signs worth a visit:

Pain at the back of the jaw

Pressure or aching behind your last molars, often coming and going.

Swollen or sore gums

A partly erupted tooth leaves an opening where bacteria cause infection.

Crowding or shifting teeth

Pressure on neighboring teeth can push them out of line — a concern after braces.

Food traps and decay

Teeth that far back are hard to brush and floss, so cavities form.

Often fine to keep

Fully erupted, upright, aligned with your bite, and easy to keep clean. We'll still watch them on future X-rays.

Usually best removed

Impacted or partly erupted teeth can lead to infection, decay, damage or cracks in nearby teeth, clenching and grinding, and occasionally cysts. Removing them earlier usually means an easier recovery.

Common questions

What age is best to remove wisdom teeth?+

Many patients come in during their late teens or early twenties, when roots are still forming and healing tends to be quicker. Adults can have them removed at any age — the consultation tells you what's right for you.

Will it hurt?+

You won't feel the surgery itself. Afterward, expect soreness and swelling for a few days. Dr. McLelland prescribes medication if you need it — take it exactly as directed.

How long is recovery?+

Most people return to school or desk work in a few days and feel close to normal within about a week. Nelva guides you through the first two weeks.

Do I need someone to drive me?+

Yes. Because sedation is used, a responsible adult must drive you to and from your surgery.

What is dry socket, and how do I avoid it?+

It happens when the blood clot in the socket is lost too early, leaving the area painful. Skip straws, smoking and hard spitting for the first several days, and follow your after-care instructions closely.
